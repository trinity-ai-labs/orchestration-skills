# AGENTS.md

Conventions for anyone — human or agent — working in this repo. `.agents/worktree.json`'s `briefConventions` points here rather than restating any of this, per the `setup` skill's own rule: a convention that lives in `AGENTS.md` gets pointed at, not copied, because a copy is a second place that can drift out of sync with the original and nobody notices which one is stale.

## Conventions

- This repo is PUBLIC — anything committed is readable by anyone, so never commit a client or engagement name, a real home path from a private machine, or any identifier belonging to a subject; worked examples in the skills stay generic.
- Every rule added to a skill must name the failure it prevents — a rule with no failure mode behind it is ceremony, and the next editor has no way to tell whether deleting it is safe.
- **A citation names its referent: never a count of it, never its position.** **Counts first — a citation of a numbered list names the list, never how many items it has.** Three moves, and they are RANKED rather than equal — take the first that applies. None of them reaches a count stated in the same sentence as the list it counts (*"all three causes below"* above a three-row table): the reader checks that by looking, so it is not a citation and nothing here asks you to strip it. **First — an item inside a numbered list cites only the items that PRECEDE it.** `skills/orchestrate/SKILL.md` §3 is the worked example: item 5 cites *items 1-4*, item 6 cites *items 1-5*, item 7 cites *items 1-5* and *item 6*, so appending an eighth falsifies none of them by construction rather than by anyone remembering to look. *The failure this prevents runs both ways, and the second direction is the one this repo actually hit: a citation reaching across the whole list must be rewritten by every insertion, and a convention that is load-bearing but unwritten gets rewritten away by someone tidying in good faith — the breakdown for §3's seventh item read those three numerals as things the insertion moved, and applying it literally would have had each item cite itself.* **Second — where the citation is remote (another paragraph, another section, another file), de-ordinalise it rather than increment it.** *The items above*, *one of the items enumerated above*, *the bullet on X* — never *the five items above* or *the third bullet*. *The failure this prevents: a stale count reads exactly as authoritative as a current one, so neither a reader nor a reviewer can tell the two apart, and there is nothing anywhere that compares them.* **Third — only where a remote count genuinely cannot be removed, declare one site authoritative, knowing that this repo has already watched that fail.** 3.19.0 did exactly that for `skills/execute/SKILL.md`'s paste-verbatim enumeration, after a count that had read wrong from 1.8.0 through 3.18.2 — and the commit that installed the rule broke it in its own diff, sweeping two of the three citing lines and leaving the third, three lines below and visible as a context line in the same hunk, reading *the five items above*, where it stayed until 3.26.0 deleted the remote counts instead. That is the ranking's whole argument: de-ordinalising removes the referent, whereas declaring an authority only relocates the obligation to remember, and the obligation is the part that fails. **Positions second — and the move there is a DISTANCE test rather than deletion.** A **positional pointer** carries no count at all — *"which is the step above"*, *"the paragraph below"*, *"see the note below"* — so none of the three moves reaches it, and it fails in the exact way they exist to prevent: the text it points at moves, the pointer still reads as authoritative, and nothing anywhere compares the two. What is different is that a pointer is usually right to KEEP. Most of the corpus's pointers sit against the element they mean, where the same-sentence carve-out's reasoning applies unchanged — the reader checks it by looking, and no insertion can move it. **So: ADJACENT stays — the element immediately before or after the pointer, with nothing of the same kind in between. Anything further is a remote citation wearing different clothes, and takes the second move: name the referent, not its position.** *The failure this prevents, observed in #138: "close it at close-out with everything else, **which is the step above**" resolved an arc-level close to the per-merge hand-close, whose timing contradicts the sentence making the reference. Both sentences were individually correct and the pointer is what made them disagree; it shipped that way across releases, and 3.27.0 fixed it by naming what it meant — `skills/orchestrate/SKILL.md` §7's termination check. Nothing in the three moves could have caught it, because there is no numeral in it to catch.* **This reaches back into the second move's own replacements, which are ordered rather than equivalent**: *the bullet on X* names the referent and holds at any distance, while *the items above* and *one of the items enumerated above* are de-ordinalised but still positional — fine where the list is adjacent, which is where they sit today, and needing the name the moment a citing line moves away from it. **It is a rule for the next pointer, not a licence to sweep**: rewriting an adjacent one is churn, and the ones already past the line are found by resolving each pointer to what it actually lands on, never by grepping for *above*. **Both halves end at the word NAME, and a numbered item is where a name and a position look identical — so: an item's own label is a name; a position within a list is not.** The test is what an insertion does. *The third bullet* is computed by counting neighbours — insert one above and the words still read while the referent has silently moved. *Invariant 8*, *§3*, *Step 4* are written AT the referent, in its own heading or bold lead, so an insertion above leaves them resolving where they did, and a renumbering that moved one would be an edit to the referent itself — citation and referent break together, in one diff. So `skills/setup/references/gate-queue.md`'s *invariant 8's retention window* and the corpus's `§N` citations of a numbered section are names and stay, while the ban on *the third bullet* is unchanged, because nothing writes *third* at that bullet. **And where a referent carries no name of its own, coining one is authoring rather than citing — expected, not a gap in the rule:** `README.md` cites *the platform table under* Prerequisites, a table with no title, and leans on the section anchor to make the coinage resolvable. *The failure this prevents runs both ways: without the distinction the ban strips citations that were already append-stable and replaces a label that resolves with a phrase that is vaguer; and read the other way, as an exemption for ordinals at large, it keeps* the third bullet *by calling it a label.* **None of this is enforced, and a green gate is not evidence for it.** `scripts/check.sh`'s citation check catches a stale *path* because a path either resolves in the tree or it does not; a count has no referent to resolve against, and a pointer's referent is the file's own layout rather than anything in the tree, so there is nothing a checker could compare either with and this stays a prose convention.
- **A citation must resolve in the repo the prose is READ in, not the one it was written in.** The convention above governs how a citation names its referent; this governs whether the reader has that referent at all. The `SKILL.md` files here SHIP, and are read by agents working inside other repos — so in that prose a bare `README.md` or `AGENTS.md` names **the reader's** repo, not this one. That is exactly right where the sentence **instructs** (*start from the repo's own doc set (`README.md`, `AGENTS.md`)*) and always wrong where it **cites**, because *this corpus argues X in `README.md`* is a claim about a file the reader does not have: true for the author, false everywhere it is actually read. So **instruct with a bare filename freely; cite only by a `skills/…/SKILL.md` path** — the one form that resolves in the repo the skill is read in, and the only one `scripts/check.sh` can check, since its citation check is deliberately blind to bare filenames and the comment at that check argues why widening it would be wrong rather than merely hard. **This is a rule about THIS repo's shipped prose, which is why it lives here** and not in `skills/decompose/SKILL.md`'s brief conventions: a project-agnostic skill stating it would assert, in every repo that skill is read in, a constraint that holds in one — the same defect wearing the other shoe. Briefs written here reach it the way they reach the rest of this file, through `.agents/worktree.json`'s `briefConventions`. *The failure this prevents, observed while dispatching #176: a decomposition comment, and the slice brief written from it, told an implementer to point a ⚠️ at two places the main-checkout rule is argued — one a `skills/…` path, the other "`README.md`'s worktree-layout paragraph". The implementer wrote both as instructed, caught the second in its own review pass, and cut it. Nothing else in the flow would have: it is not a count, not a position, and it resolves perfectly for anyone checking it from this checkout.*
- **A count states what it counts, or it is checkable by looking.** The two conventions above govern what a citation may **name**; this governs what a **number** has to carry to be a claim at all, and the line between them is the same-sentence case they already carve out. That carve-out is the whole of what needs nothing and it is not narrowed here: *"all three causes below"* above a three-row table, *"eight files today"* beside the directory that holds them — the reader checks it by looking, no insertion can move it, and a count a reader can verify by looking is fine. What this reaches is the other kind: **a count nobody can reproduce, because the instrument is unnamed.** The cheapest instrument in this corpus returns two different true numbers over the same sweep, the same word and the same referent — `grep -c` counts the **lines containing** a term, `grep -o | wc -l` counts its **occurrences** — and they diverge on every passage that names its subject more than once, which here is the ordinary case, since a rule and the failure it prevents are written in one bullet. So **name the unit, or name the command**: *7 lines*, *18 occurrences*, *4 seats*, never a bare numeral. **Prefer the count of the thing being counted**, carrying a sweep's own count beside it as the evidence that produced it rather than as the claim. **A comparison names its baseline too**, because two counts of one quantity taken against two trees are not comparable however fixed the unit is. **And a measurement of the observer's own environment can satisfy neither, so it does not belong in shipped prose at all** — the referent is a directory on whichever machine ran the measurement, so no reader can reproduce it, no editor can sweep it, and it goes stale on a schedule nothing in this repository controls; drop it and keep the claim it was supporting, which is what the argument was resting on anyway. *The failure this prevents: a count is the one part of an enumeration a later reader can check **without redoing the work**, which is the whole reason the every-seat rule demands one — and a unitless count cannot be checked at all, because a reader who re-measures and gets a different number cannot tell a short enumeration from a different instrument. It is worse for the consumer than for the author, who at least knows which command they ran. Observed: a grounding table's column headed* seam occurrences *carried `5 / 7 / 7 / 0`, which are line counts; the occurrence counts over the identical sweep are `7 / 18 / 11 / 0`. Both sets were correct, and the brief written from that table told its implementer to re-measure and trust its own numbers where they differed — an instruction with no slot for a disagreement that is a unit rather than a discrepancy.* **It is a rule for the next count, not a licence to sweep**: the ones already in the corpus are found by resolving each to the instrument that produced it, never by grepping for numerals.
- The shipped/contributor split is a directory: everything under `bin/` SHIPS — eight files today, four helpers (`setup-worktree`, `merge-pr`, `remove-worktree`, `setup-workspace`) each shipped twice, once as `.sh` and once as `.ps1` — because Claude Code puts an enabled plugin's `bin/` on PATH for whichever shell tool it hands the user, so skills invoke those helpers bare, never by path. Which half lands on PATH depends on the shell: the Bash tool (WSL, or Git Bash on native Windows) gets the `.sh` half, but a native Windows install with no Git for Windows gets the PowerShell tool instead, where the `.sh` helpers do not exist at all — that gap is the entire reason the `.ps1` ports were written. A bug in either half is a runtime failure discovered on a user's machine at the moment of use. For the `.sh` half, they are bash, and shellcheck reads the dialect from each shebang, so the shebang is the contract and changing it changes what the gate enforces. For the `.ps1` half the equivalent guarantee is PSScriptAnalyzer — run in CI's `check` job on `ubuntu-latest`, which ships `pwsh` and the analyzer preinstalled, and locally by `scripts/check.sh` when `pwsh` is on PATH — together with the parity check that holds both languages to the same contract.
- A `.ps1` helper DECLARES the session state it relies on rather than inheriting it. These scripts run in a child scope of whatever session invoked them, so a preference variable set in a user's profile is in force inside them — which is why `$PSNativeCommandUseErrorActionPreference` is pinned `$false` beside each helper's `$ErrorActionPreference = 'Stop'`, and why a possibly-absent JSON property is read through a `Get-JsonValue` wrapper rather than directly, since `Set-StrictMode 2.0` turns the plain form into a throw. Neither setting is part of the frozen contract below — they are internal to each script, and adding one to the parity check's contract env list would be widening the contract to describe an implementation detail. The failure this prevents: these helpers spend a non-zero exit as a *question* throughout — `git show-ref --verify` asking whether a branch exists, `merge --ff-only` asking whether a fast-forward is possible — so a session with native-command errors switched on makes the probe itself fatal and every hand-rolled `$LASTEXITCODE` check unreachable. Nothing in the gate can see that coming: PSScriptAnalyzer does not model session state, and CI exercises one `pwsh` configuration on `ubuntu-latest`, so a break of this kind reaches the machine whose profile differs and nowhere earlier — the same "discovered on a user's machine at the moment of use" cost the parity rule exists for, arriving through configuration instead of through a missing file.
- Never game a guardrail — a check that fires is a signal to fix the code, not a number to route around — and this repo ships one sanctioned exception to that. `bin/setup-worktree.ps1`'s `Invoke-ProjectInstall` carries a one-function `SuppressMessageAttribute` for `PSAvoidUsingInvokeExpression`, with its reasoning in the `Justification` and in the comment above it; read those rather than a paraphrase of them here. It is load-bearing: do not delete it to comply with the rule, and do not cite it as precedent for silencing an analyzer that found something real. The documented-exception carve-out it satisfies, and the four conditions any new suppression must meet, are defined in `skills/execute/SKILL.md` — pointed at, not restated, because a three-condition copy here would be worse than none. The one thing to know locally: a repo-wide PSScriptAnalyzer settings-file exclusion is never the answer, since it switches the rule off everywhere and leaves nothing at the flagged code to show it was ever flagged. The failure this prevents runs both ways — a contributor who reads the existing suppression as permission adds an unjustified one beside it, and a contributor who reads the rule as unqualified deletes the load-bearing one and turns CI red.
- Everything under `scripts/` is contributor-only — never loaded, never on a user's PATH — and `scripts/check.sh` is POSIX `/bin/sh` with no bashisms so it holds to the standard it enforces on `bin/`.
- The repo is ZERO-dependency by design: no `package.json`, no lockfile, no install step, no env files. That is why a worktree here is fully functional bare, and why adding a dependency would make the gate unrunnable for anyone who has not first installed one.
- `gate` and `scopedCheck` are the same command and there is no queue — run `sh scripts/check.sh` green yourself, then open a PR into the integration branch. **Open it as a DRAFT and stop there when nobody has authorized the merge**: the draft is your hand-back, the reviewer flips it ready, and GitHub's refusal to merge a draft is the interlock against merging an unread diff. **A maintainer saying "ship it" IS that authorization, and it is not a request for a draft** — carry the same path all the way through instead: version bump, green gate, push, PR, flip it ready, merge it (merge commit, never squash), and bring the local integration branch up to the merged tip. *The failure this prevents runs both ways, which is why the endpoint is named rather than left to judgement. An agent that merges on its own initiative spends the only review artifact a prose repo has — no gate here can tell whether a rule is CORRECT, so the diff is the review. And an agent that hands back a draft after being told to ship leaves a half-done release wearing every appearance of a finished one: the version is bumped, the checks are green, the PR is open, and nothing is actually shipped until the maintainer comes back and finishes it by hand — which is the exact cost the instruction was spent to avoid.*
- Assume your change ships and needs the version in `.claude-plugin/plugin.json` moved forward plus a matching `## <version>` heading in `CHANGELOG.md` — an install is pinned to that string, so merging a skill change under an unchanged version ships nothing while looking like it worked. Only `.github/`, `.agents/`, `scripts/`, `CHANGELOG.md` and `.gitignore` are exempt (the exempt regex in `.github/workflows/ci.yml` is authoritative — change both together).
- All three `trinity-ai-labs` skills repos — `market-skills`, `orchestration-skills`, `framework-skills` — are PR-only, never a direct push to `main`, docs and CHANGELOG included: in a repo whose product is prose no gate can tell whether a rule is CORRECT, so the diff is the only review artifact there is and a direct push spends it to save a worktree.
- Never rebase. Merge commits, not squash. **Never self-merge on your own judgement** — that is the unauthorized half of the failure above. A merge a maintainer explicitly asked for is not a self-merge: it is the review arriving as a sentence rather than as a checkbox, and treating it as one is how a "ship it" turns back into a draft.

## The frozen helper contract

The helper CLI contract — arguments, env vars (`WORKTREE_HOME`, `REPO`, `WORKSPACE`, `WORKTREE_DEST`, `MERGE_PR_FORCE`), the stdout lines, and exit codes — is exactly as it is on `main` today, and stays frozen: no one may add, rename, or repurpose any of them without stopping and re-agreeing first.

As it stands: `setup-worktree` takes `<branch> <base>` to fork a new branch, or `--existing <branch>` to attach a worktree to a branch that is already there (no base — an existing branch's base is whatever it already forked from), and prints `READY: <path>` followed by `HEAD: <sha>`, the worktree's resulting commit. `merge-pr` takes `<pr-number>`; `remove-worktree` takes a branch leaf or an absolute path; `setup-workspace` takes `[--dry-run] <branch> [repo …]` or `[--dry-run] <branch> --exclude <repo,repo>`, and prints `READY: <path>`. The `READY:` line's shape is what callers parse, so an addition to a helper's output goes on its OWN line rather than into that one.

The failure this prevents: this repo ships the same behavior twice, once as bash (`bin/*.sh`) and once as PowerShell (`bin/*.ps1`), built and maintained against a shared contract neither implementation owns alone. If one side changes what an argument, env var, the `READY:` line, or an exit code *means* without the other side knowing, the two stop agreeing on the same input, and nothing catches it — the parity check in `scripts/check.sh` only reads surface shape (does the sibling exist, do the usage lines and env-var names match, is it ASCII, does a helper that resolves paths under `WORKTREE_HOME` read `.agents/workspace.json` at all), not semantics, so a silently repurposed flag ships as a clean green check on both sides while the two shells behave differently for the same command. A contract change that's actually needed is still fine — it just has to be a conversation both implementations sign off on, not a unilateral edit discovered later as a behavioral mismatch.

## The `bin/` parity rule

Every `bin/<name>.sh` must have a `bin/<name>.ps1` sibling with the same usage line and the same set of consumed env vars, and neither sibling may ever be added alone.

The failure this prevents: `bin/` ships on a user's PATH inside whichever shell Claude Code hands them for that platform — the Bash tool (WSL, or Git Bash on native Windows) or the PowerShell tool (native Windows with no Git for Windows installed, where bash does not exist at all). A helper that exists in only one language works for part of the userbase and is simply unavailable to the rest, with no error until a user on the missing shell tries to run it and finds nothing on PATH.

`.ps1` files are ASCII-only, no exceptions. PowerShell 5.1 misreads UTF-8 without a byte-order mark, and these helpers' comments lean on em-dashes throughout — a single stray em-dash surviving into a `.ps1` file is a non-ASCII byte that corrupts under 5.1, and the failure surfaces as a parse error nowhere near the character that actually caused it, which makes it expensive to trace back.

This rule is enforced mechanically, not carried by review discipline — prose alone does not stop two hand-maintained copies of the same logic from drifting apart, it only makes the drift someone's fault after the fact. `scripts/check.sh`'s parity check fails closed on: a missing sibling in either direction; a usage-line mismatch, read from each script's runtime `usage:` line rather than its comments; a mismatch in which CONTRACT env vars (`WORKTREE_HOME`, `REPO`, `WORKSPACE`, `WORKTREE_DEST`, `MERGE_PR_FORCE`) each sibling consumes — deliberately not every variable either side internally touches, since the bash helpers carry MSYS path-translation plumbing PowerShell has no counterpart for; and a non-ASCII byte or missing trailing newline in a `.ps1` file. It also asserts one thing about each sibling on its own, because comparing the two is structurally blind to an omission they SHARE: a helper that consumes `WORKTREE_HOME` must read `.agents/workspace.json`, since a repo inside a workspace keeps its worktrees at `$WORKTREE_HOME/<workspace>/<leaf>/<repo>` rather than the bare `$WORKTREE_HOME/<project>/<leaf>`. That is still surface shape — a token in the comment-stripped source, exactly like the env-var scan — and it makes no claim that the branch it finds is *correct*, only that the question was asked; whether a workspace path is assembled right is semantics, and a checker that judged it would be a second implementation of the thing it checks. The failure it prevents, observed: `remove-worktree` shipped with no workspace branch in **either** language, so both ports agreed with each other perfectly while every workspace member's teardown resolved a path that never exists, printed "already removed", and exited 0 — after which `merge-pr`'s `--delete-branch` ran against a branch still checked out in a live worktree.

## The every-seat rule

A rule this corpus states to more than one role is one change, however many files that takes. A **seat** is one role's statement of a constraint, sitting where that role reads — and a rule here routinely has three or four of them rather than two, while a single file can hold more than one, as `skills/execute/SKILL.md` holds a dispatcher's seat and an implementer's. **When a change adds or alters a constraint that another seat already speaks to, the question to answer is not whether there is another seat — it is WHICH seats state this constraint, and the answer is an enumeration you produce and write down, never a match you stop at.** Every seat that enumeration returns is in scope for the change, and they are read against each other before any of them lands. The trigger is mechanical — the same subject addressed to more than one role — and what it forbids is landing one seat's statement on the grounds that the file it sits in is internally consistent, because internal consistency is exactly what a scattered rule has.

**Enumerate rather than find, and this corpus already argues why, one file over.** `skills/decompose/SKILL.md` Step 1 makes the same move for a project's enforcement chains — *find every declaration, not the first*, because stopping at the first hit leaves the rest silently pointing at something that is gone — and its *An enumeration is a claim* is why the count travels with the list, since a set has nothing in it to say it is short. This rule is that reasoning turned on the corpus's own rules rather than on a project's code. So write the seats down with how many there are, and write down the ones you decided need nothing: a seat scoped out from the plan rather than from the file is a claim, and it is one the next reader can overturn only if it was recorded. *The failure this prevents is Step 1's own, arriving in prose instead of in an enforcement chain: an enumeration that stopped early is internally consistent — every seat it names is real, and every seat it missed is silently unchanged — so nothing in the short list, or in the change built from it, reads as short.*

**And that number is a count of SEATS — say so, because a sweep's own count is a different number over the same subject.** Neither the count of lines a pattern matched nor the count of times it occurs is the count of seats, and those two grep units are not each other either; carry whatever a sweep returned beside the list as the evidence that produced it, named with the instrument, and let the claim be the seats. The Conventions bullet on counts is the general form. *The failure this prevents: the seat count is the one thing a reviewer can check against the diff without redoing the enumeration, and a bare numeral beside a list of seats is read as a seat count whichever number it actually was — so a sweep's hit count, routinely the larger, reads as an enumeration wider than the one that was performed, and it reads that way in the PR body, which is exactly where the checking is supposed to happen.*

**How you KEY that enumeration is the half this rule kept leaving open, and the cheap method fails in both directions.** Requiring a written list with a count in front of it makes the cheapest way to produce one a single `grep` for the constraint's distinctive wording — so the remedy recommends the defect. That sweep **undercounts**: a seat stating the constraint in other words is invisible to a search on any one wording, and the differently-worded seat is disproportionately the one that matters, because it was written that way for a different reader. It also **overcounts**: a word the constraint happens to use names a different referent elsewhere in the corpus, and those hits enter the list as seats that were never seats. Both directions produce a confident, well-formed enumeration and neither errors, which is why the count beside the list cannot rescue either. **So: a single-pattern sweep is not an enumeration**, however faithfully its output is written down.

**And the cheapest way for a sweep to miss a seat is not different wording at all — it is the SAME wording, cased differently.** A constraint stated mid-sentence in one seat and at the start of a sentence in another differs by one letter, so a case-sensitive pattern returns the first, misses the second, and reads as a complete answer either way. That is strictly cheaper to hit than the differently-worded seat above, because there is nothing to notice: the two seats say the same words. *The failure this prevents, observed on the brief that dispatched this very change. It reported that one seat of this corpus stated a machine-local count and that two others reached the same conclusion "without stating any number at all", and told its implementer to verify that rather than take it. All three state the number; the two it called clean open their sentences with it, and the sweep behind the claim was the lowercase phrase. The brief was **correcting** the filed issue's own count when it did this — so a case-sensitive grep produced a confident contradiction of a claim that was right, in a brief whose subject was counts, and the verify bar it shipped (`grep -c "three newer versions"`) was blind to the same two seats and would have gone green with both of them untouched.*

**The method that answers it is the remaining half of the step this rule already borrows from.** `skills/decompose/SKILL.md` Step 1 tells a decomposer to *derive them from the behavior, never from a keyword grep*, and gives the reason — prose describing a thing in plain language contains none of the terms a change introduces, by construction, so the grep was always going to come back empty — and then to read the candidates rather than grepping them. A seat set has exactly that property, so the same method transfers: **state the constraint in your own words first, then search for its subject from more than one direction** — the symptom it prevents, the condition it fires on, the consequence it forbids — and reconcile what those sweeps return against each other rather than trusting whichever ran first. **Read every candidate before it enters the enumeration**, because nothing about a hit distinguishes a seat from a coincidence of vocabulary, and only reading separates them. And **record the candidates you excluded together with the referent they actually named**, so an exclusion is a claim the next reader can overturn rather than an absence they cannot see.

*The failure this prevents, observed three times in one day and twice within a single arc. One slice's enumeration returned four seats where there were seven, the three it missed sitting in files nobody had thought to search. A dispatcher enumerated one constraint at four seats in one file where there were eight — and the four it missed included both the emphatic sentence that produced the very failure being repaired and two enumerations elsewhere in that same file that were themselves silently short by exactly the case under repair. The same dispatcher, in the same arc, overcounted the other constraint by naming two files on the strength of a single word, every occurrence of which named the gate queue's settlement directory rather than the subject being enumerated. That dispatcher had warned its own implementer against keying on a wording, in the brief that then did it — which is the tell that this survives knowing about it: the method that is wrong is also the only one that is cheap.*

Two deliberate properties of this corpus scatter a rule across seats in the first place, and neither of them is the defect. It divides its rules by role — a planning pass, a dispatch pass, an implementation pass — so no reader carries rules written for somebody else's seat. And it points between files rather than restating, because copies drift and nothing marks which of them is stale. What they create jointly is a rule **no single reader holds all of**, with nothing in the flow comparing the seats: an implementer's Step 0 loads `skills/execute/SKILL.md` and loads neither `skills/decompose/SKILL.md` nor `skills/orchestrate/SKILL.md`, so a decomposer can write a carve-out into the field it owns while the only file the acting party opens says that carve-out cannot exist.

**Asking about a pair is what leaves the third and fourth seats empty, and it fails while looking like the rule working.** A pair is what a reader looks for once prompted, so the pair-shaped question gets answered correctly and often: a genuine second seat is found, the missing statement is written, and the change lands as a clean fix in one PR. What that reader never asked is which *other* seats state the constraint. *The failure this prevents, observed within a single increment of 3.44.0. A tie-break on a handed-down measured value landed in the dispatch and implementer seats of `skills/execute/SKILL.md` and, in that release, in no other seat — not the grounding pass, which also emits briefs, and which is the seat where it mattered most, because a brief written there is the one that has to carry the tie-break before the disagreement it settles. In the same increment, a rule about which copy of these skills a pass reads its own governing prose from landed in that same file's seats and in no others, while the grounding pass and the loop, which read from that same stale copy, got nothing. Both gaps were found by an implementer after the change had been scoped, and filed as follow-ups rather than caught here.*

The failure is silent in the direction that matters, and a partly-landed rule reads as **more** settled than an unlanded one. Every seat that got the rule obeys it and every seat that did not is violating nothing, so no reader anywhere meets a contradiction; and the corpus now holds a correct, well-argued statement of the rule, so whoever finds that statement has no reason to check whether the remaining seats have it too. It surfaces only when someone happens to hold every seat at once, which is why the instances caught so far were each caught late and each treated as a one-off: a verify bar's fork-point baseline that never reached the implementer; a brief that may say *derive X from Y* where Y is unreachable from the slice, whose compliant move is silent transcription; an enumeration rule binding the dispatcher and not the party that runs the same commands; a config-read remedy reaching only the implementer; a fence disposition written for the decomposer while the implementer's own file said a fence leaves one move. Each was filed as its own defect, fixed by filling the seat it named, and closed — and not one of them names the others.

**Nothing enforces this, and nothing here can.** The `bin/` parity rule is enforced mechanically and states exactly where that enforcement stops, at semantics — a checker that judged meaning would be a second implementation of the thing it checks. This rule sits entirely on the far side of that line: whether the statements addressed to several roles agree is not a comparison any check can make, so `scripts/check.sh` is silent on it by construction, and a green gate on the PR that lands a rule in one seat is exactly as green as one on the PR that lands it in all of them. What that costs is what the parity rule already says prose costs — it does not stop the drift, it only makes the drift someone's fault after the fact — with the difference that here there is no mechanical layer underneath catching the shape while review catches the meaning. The whole of it is review, so the obligation is the author's: enumerate the seats in the change that touches one of them and read them against each other, or the seats go unread and the next reader to hold them all finds the gap by accident, arcs later, in a file that change never touched.

**And because the whole of it is review, the enumeration goes in the PR body, where the reviewer who decides the change is complete is already reading.** Until now this rule mandated a record and named nowhere to keep it, which is the defect it exists to prevent arriving one level up — every comparable record in this corpus has both a home and a party keyed on reading it, and an enumeration that lives only in an author's reasoning has neither. The test is which party needs it and when, and that settles it: the enumeration is a claim about what the diff does **not** contain — that the seats absent from it were found, read, and deliberately scoped out — so it has to reach whoever weighs the diff, at the moment they weigh it, and no earlier artifact can carry it. A hand-back is the natural place to draft it and is not a home, because it ends with the turn. A brief cannot hold it, because the brief is written before the enumeration exists and is the thing the enumeration most often corrects. The umbrella holds the arc's state and is rewritten each cycle, where this is a claim about one diff that has to outlive the arc alongside it. So: the seats, how many there are, and the ones scoped out with the referent each actually named, in the body of the PR that lands the change — and where a dispatcher's enumeration scoped the slice, the implementer's own re-derivation belongs in the same place, since a scope that came back short is a finding about the brief rather than a boundary to respect.

**And the reader's half, because an obligation to write with no obligation to read is the defect this rule exists to name, arriving one level up in its own fix.** The reviewer who decides the change is complete reads that enumeration against the diff rather than accepting it — the way `skills/execute/SKILL.md` already has a dispatcher read the per-doc verdict against the diff rather than accepting it — because an author can only enumerate the seats it thought to look for, and the seats it missed are by construction the ones stated in words it did not search for. **What to do when the PR body carries none is the half that decides whether any of this works, because a change that needed no enumeration and a change where nobody asked are the same blank page.** So apply the trigger yourself, from the diff, instead of inferring it from the record: does this change add or alter a constraint this corpus states to more than one role? Where the answer is no the blank is correct and there is nothing to want. Where it is yes, a missing enumeration is a round trip and not a merge blocker to argue about — and a present one is read for the two things a reader can check without redoing the work: that it carries the count the sweep returned, and that every candidate it left out is recorded against the referent that candidate actually named. *The failure this prevents: nothing in the gate can see any of this, by construction, so the enumeration's only reader is the party deciding to merge — and a record whose only reader is not obliged to look is what this corpus calls recorded rather than filed. Landing the author's half alone leaves this rule stated in exactly one of its own seats, which is the shape it was written to catch.*

**State it in whatever words the change calls for — there is no fixed form here, deliberately.** `Filed from behind a fence:` is a fixed line precisely because `skills/orchestrate/SKILL.md` §4 sorts a whole class of finding by its presence, and a marker another pass recognises by the line itself has to be spelled the one way or the recognition silently fails. This record's reader is a reviewer, so what is required of it is content and not spelling: a reader can tell whether a list names its seats, carries its count, and accounts for what it left out, and cannot be helped by a heading. Fixing the wording would also cost more than it bought, since sibling changes landing under this rule at the same time cannot see each other's prose, and a form each had to match verbatim is one none of them could have read. *The failure this prevents: a change that landed one seat and never asked about the others is indistinguishable — in the diff, in the PR, and in the gate, which is silent on this by construction — from one that enumerated four seats and correctly landed the one that needed changing. Both are internally consistent, both are green, and the record that separates them is the only thing that ever could.*
